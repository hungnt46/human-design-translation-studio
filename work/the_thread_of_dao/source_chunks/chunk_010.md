<span style=" font-size:1.0rem">     Thus, it appears that the proto-Daoist</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">texts, though found in what is generally considered a Legalist compilation, were in large part written in response to the Legalist ideologies of Shang Yang, prevalent at the time it was most likely written, around 350 BC. The writersâ€™ attempts to warn Legalists of the imbalances that would result from such stringent adherence to their doctrines turned prophetic when the Qin Dynasty, bolstered by the Legalist policies of Shang Yang earlier on, swept all of China by 221 BC, and then fell in 206 BC, only 15 years after unification.</span>

<span style=" font-size:1.0rem">     Much of the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">contains advice more closely resembling that of Confucius and Lao Zi than the advice of Shang Yang. The writers of the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">advise considering the welfare of the people as most important, and instilling righteousness, benevolence, frugality, and simplicity in them, rather than simply manipulating them with rewards and punishments, laws, and even poverty as Shang Yang does. While the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Bai Xin, Xin Shu</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">(Shang and Xia), and</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Nei Ye</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">are its only texts on Daoist internal cultivation, a great deal of the writings on statecraft in</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">seem to heed Lao Ziâ€™s teachings in the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Dao De Jing</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">, though they may have been transcribed at an earlier date. If we are to faithfully ascribe these writings to Guan Zi and Lao Zi, Guan Zi (720-645 BC) would have written his texts some 100 years before Lao Zi wrote the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Dao De Jing</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">. Perhaps, as suggested earlier, the historical thread of these teachings goes back even further to the shamans and sages of the Shang (1600-1046 BC) or Xia (2070-1600 BC) Dynasties.</span>

<span style=" font-size:1.0rem">     Because of Qin Shi Huangâ€™s order to burn all philosophical books in 213 BC, it is difficult to say with any certainty when the Daoist approach to government first appeared in writing. The influence of Zhou rites and culture on King Zhuang of Chu shows that such ideals for government, and virtuous rule, were in place long before Confuciusâ€™ time, even if fidelity to these ideals was not constant throughout the Zhou Dynasty. Confucius, who helped to revitalize these ideals, was likely a product of the zeitgeist of his time, realizing the precipitous nature of an empire losing its principles while its states had begun declaring themselves kingdoms. Though the virtuous rule touted in the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">is considered Confucian in many respects, Confucius was a teacher of Zhou culture, and the philosophy and rites of Zhou are what Li Er (aka Li Dan, Lao Zi) is said to have taught while working in the Zhou imperial library. Seeing this blend of Confucianism and Daoism in the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">, then, one has to question if this approach to government is not simply a product of studying Zhou culture, a subject which comprised the education of the nobility for hundreds of years prior to Confucius. That Confucius did not even leave his home state of Lu until he was in his 50s, and yet still knew so much about Zhou culture, shows that the influence of Zhou-centered education was widespread, and likely well documented until â€œthe burning of the booksâ€? during the Qin Dynasty.</span>

<span style=" font-size:1.0rem">     Another question raised by the seemingly Daoist centered approach to government in</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">, pertains to its relationship to Daoist meditation. Much of the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Dao De Jing</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">and the proto-Daoist texts in</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">use metaphors of pacifying the nation when describing how to bring about inner peace. These writings suggest that by bringing order and peace to oneself through non-effort, minimizing desires, and utilizing the course of nature, one will then understand how to bring order and peace to a nation. Though this process begins by finding inner peace, it is nonetheless difficult to say for certain if this approach to meditation inspired the corresponding approach to government, or if the corresponding approach to government enlightened the Sages as to its facility in cultivating the inner domain.</span>

<span style=" font-size:1.0rem">     Much of the inter-textual analysis in</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Thread of Dao</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">focuses on the complementary relationship between the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Dao De Jing</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">and proto-Daoist</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">texts, as they expound in subtler and more overt ways on the same principles of statecraft and internal cultivation.</span> <span id="_ftnref26"></span> [<span style=" font-size:0.67rem; vertical-align:super">\[26\]</span>](text00000.html#_ftn26) <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">It appears, at times, that the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Dao De Jing</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">pre-supposed a familiarity with the concepts and principles in the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">, and was written to clarify the relationship between statecraft and internal cultivation. At other times, it appears that the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Dao De Jing</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">describes, more esoterically, elements of statecraft that the</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem; font-style:italic">Guan Zi</span> <span style=" font-size:1.0rem">texts present more clearly as elements of internal cultivation. This, again, makes it difficult to determine which application of these principles came first â€“ the skillful application of Dao and De in statecraft, or internal cultivation.</span>
